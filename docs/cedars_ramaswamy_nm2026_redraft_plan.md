**Design facts (abstract + full-text methods excerpts + PI full-text enrichment on the dashboard):**

- Tested the **live ChatGPT Health consumer interface** (gpt-5-mini "thinking" backbone) over a three-day window, 2026-01-09 to 01-11 — a single non-configurable product deployment at a single timepoint.

- Triage scale: 4-level Likert — A "monitor at home" / B "see a doctor within weeks" / C "see a doctor within 24–48 h" / D "go to the emergency department." The ceiling is ED self-transport; EMS activation is not distinguished.

## 2. Strategic read: validation, not a scoop

The paper proves the premise and leaves the instrument unbuilt. Every differentiator on our list survives contact with their methods, and three of their acknowledged limitations are literally arms or design features of this protocol (physician answering benchmark; human–LLM interaction arm; repeated runs / ongoing multi-model evaluation). Their closing sentence is an argument for funding this trial.

The risk it creates is rhetorical, not scientific: Farkouh and Spiegel will likely know this paper (Mount Sinai; Farkouh's former institution), and every reviewer will ask "how does this differ from Ramaswamy?" That answer must be pre-drafted in the protocol background, the methods, the deck, and the Friday talking points — never improvised.

The narrative upgrade is the **three-pillar structure**:

1. **Allegation** — *Winters v. OpenAI*: the disposition-failure phenotype in the wild (reassurance, invented threshold, advised immobility). Still presented as allegations.
2. **Measurement — now contested** — Ramaswamy: the phenotype registered under controlled conditions in the deployed product (51.6% of gold-standard emergencies undertriaged; anchoring OR 11.7; crisis safeguards unpredictable) — with a replication attributing much of the failure to the evaluation format itself (§2.5).
3. **Interaction gap** — Bean / Goh / Healy: whatever a model does alone degrades further in lay users' hands.

An allegation, a contested measurement, and an interaction gap — and still no standard instrument. This protocol supplies it, and (per §2.5) is positioned to adjudicate the contest.

## 2.5 The open alert: Fraile Navarro et al. — the format-artifact challenge (VERIFIED)

**Citation.** Fraile Navarro D, Magrabi F, Coiera E (Macquarie University). Evaluation format, not model capability, drives triage failure in the assessment of consumer health AI. *arXiv* preprint, [10.48550/arxiv.2603.11413](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2603.11413). **Not peer-reviewed.** Verified via Scite (arXiv-indexed abstract + full-text citation snippets) 2026-08-04. Flagged on the reference-library dashboard 2026-08-03 as an urgent alert; **unresolved** as of this writing.

**Verified claims:**

- Partial replication of Ramaswamy (17-scenario bank, not the full 60) on five frontier API models (GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Pro): constrained exam-style forced A/B/C/D (1,275 trials) vs naturalistic patient-style free-text messages (850 trials), with targeted ablations and prompt-faithful checks using Ramaswamy's released prompts.
- Naturalistic interaction improved triage accuracy by 6.4 percentage points (p = 0.015). **DKA was triaged correctly in 100% of trials across all models and both conditions.** Asthma improved 48% → 80%. Three models scored 0–24% under forced choice but 100% with free text on matched scenarios (all p < 10⁻⁸) — "consistently recommending emergency care in their own words while the forced-choice format registered under-triage."
- Conclusion: the headline under-triage rate is highly contingent on evaluation format and "may not generalize as a stable estimate of deployed triage behavior."

**Verified caveats that temper the critique (from the dashboard's PI-enriched cross-check and the preprint's own limitations):**

1. **Model confound** — they tested newer, larger frontier models via API because ChatGPT Health was region-blocked; Ramaswamy tested the actual product (gpt-5-mini backbone). Format effect and model capability are conflated; it is not a clean same-system comparison.
2. **Ramaswamy's conservative-test rebuttal** — real consumers under-report symptoms and misapply advice, so clean vignettes may *understate* real-world failure. The two critiques address different axes (output format vs input quality) and can both hold; the net deployed-behavior effect is unresolved.
3. Preprint status and partial replication (17 of 60 scenarios).
4. Bean et al.'s peer-reviewed RCT keeps naturalistic optimism in check: lay users interacting freely with models still fell below 44.2% disposition accuracy — naturalistic format alone does not make consumer triage safe.

**Consequences for the redraft — this dispute is an asset, not a threat:**

- **(a) Citation discipline.** Everywhere Ramaswamy's rates appear (protocol, deck, manuscript), label them as obtained *under constrained forced-choice testing of the deployed product*, never as stable estimates of deployed behavior. A reviewer who knows the preprint will catch unqualified use of 52%.
- **(b) The trial becomes the adjudicator.** The field is now publicly arguing about **how to measure** consumer triage safety. This protocol's design answers both sides simultaneously: free-response output scored by a deterministic escalation-phrase rule set (Addendum A's hardened Change 7 scores what the model says *in its own words* — precisely what Fraile Navarro says forced choice destroys), dual lay/expert register (isolating input-quality effects — Ramaswamy's axis), multi-model + version pinning (dissolving the model-vs-product confound), repeated runs (their single-response design), and an interaction arm (the axis neither tested). Say this explicitly in the protocol and on a slide: the dispute is the strongest argument yet that the instrument this trial builds is needed *now*.
- **(c) Drop the DKA poster child.** Do not use "DKA sent to 24–48 h evaluation" as the standalone undertriage example — the replication found DKA correctly triaged 100% of the time via API free-text. Use "impending respiratory failure," or better, present the controversy itself as the exhibit.
- **(d) Matched-pair contrasts survive the critique better than absolute rates.** The anchoring OR 11.7 was estimated *within* the same format in both arms (within-vignette design), so format artifacts largely cancel in the contrast even if absolute rates are inflated. This is one more argument for Change 12's matched-pair McNemar design over absolute-rate comparisons — add a sentence to the statistics section saying so.
- **(e) Arm C1 relevance rises.** Fraile Navarro's argument that real consumer interactions are multi-turn with persistent context is exactly the longitudinal Arm C1 rationale — another point for keeping it in scope.

**Sweep findings that sharpen this dispute (added Rev. 5, 2026-08-05; all abstract-verified via Scite — read full texts before heavy citation):**

- **The multi-turn objection now has an empirical answer — from the Ramaswamy group itself.** Tyagi, Taylor, Jayaraman et al. (openRxiv preprint, 2026-07-23, doi:10.64898/2026.07.21.26358588; Mount Sinai) tested ChatGPT Health single-turn vs multi-turn on 255 cases (39 vignettes + 76 real ED + 140 nurse-line): exact agreement with nurse-line standards was 52.9% single-turn vs 55.7% multi-turn, and ~70% of discordant recommendations were UNDER-triage in both conditions. Conversational interaction did not ensure safe disposition alignment. This directly counters Fraile Navarro's core real-world argument and further supports Arm C/C1. Cite alongside §2.5 wherever the dispute appears.
- **Same format, different product, different failure profile.** Jia, Omar, Barash et al. (openRxiv preprint, 2026-04-24, doi:10.64898/2026.04.23.26351526; includes Ramaswamy co-authors) ran the identical 960-prompt benchmark against OpenEvidence (physician-facing): 12.5% emergency undertriage (four-fold lower), no anchoring effect, errors skewed safe (68.0% overtriage of Home cases), 6.8% refusals confined to symptom-only prompts. Two implications: the forced format alone does not doom performance (product/system configuration matters — supports our configuration factor and multi-model design), and refusal/abstain behavior is a real operating point (connects to the Addendum A abstain-or-escalate design norm).
- **Killer-item failure observed in the wild.** Auger & Scott (openRxiv preprint, 2026-03-25, doi:10.64898/2026.03.23.26349082): 1,000 synthetic headache transcripts; GPT-5.2 hit 97.5% diagnostic accuracy with full histories yet, with incomplete transcripts, discouraged 100% of lumbar punctures in SAH cases and downgraded life/sight-threatening emergencies to self-care/routine follow-up in up to 54.8% (GPT-5-mini); GPT-5.2 triage less safe for females (OR 3.2, 95% CI 1.4–7.1). This is the accuracy-safety dissociation and the SAH killer-item archetype, at scale; the sex-disparity signal contrasts with Ramaswamy's null (their full text: undertriage 17.0% Black vs 14.3% white patients, OR 1.96, 95% CI 0.51–7.53, Holm P = 1.0 — nulls with wide CIs, which is the §3.5 matched-pair power argument in the wild). Also claims ChatGPT Health serves ~250M weekly users on a 'mini' backbone (their sourcing — verify before citing that figure).

## 2.7 NOHARM — the judging-architecture question (VERIFIED; newly added, PI-supplied)

**Citation.** Wu D, Nateghi Haredasht F, Kumar Maharaj S, et al. (co-senior: Rodman A, Chen JH, Goh E — the same Stanford/BIDMC group as Goh et al. 2024, already in the verified ledger). First, do NOHARM: a medical safety benchmark and randomized study of physician and AI teaming on clinical consultations. *arXiv* preprint, [10.48550/arxiv.2512.01241](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2512.01241). **Not peer-reviewed.** Verified via Scite (arXiv-indexed; 7 citing publications; no editorial notices) and read in full from the PI-supplied PDF on 2026-08-05. Testing window April–June 2026; public case set released 2026-06-16; RCT under Stanford IRB #85610. **Status before this revision: absent from the protocol, the April packet, Amendment 1/Addendum A, and the 113-reference library — a scan blind spot (consultation-safety rather than triage keywords; note the v1 preprint HAS been PubMed-indexed since December 2025, so the miss was keyword scope, not indexing).**

**Version reconciliation (verified via PubMed 2026-08-05 — supersedes the scan's "unverified" flag).** arXiv 2512.01241 exists in two versions under one DOI. **v1** (posted 2025-12-17; title "First, do NOHARM: towards clinically safe large language models"; **PMID 41532042, PMC12794822** — a PubMed preprint record): 31 LLMs, no RCT, no RAG tools; severe-harm potential up to **22.2%** (95% CI 21.6–22.8%); omission **76.6%** (95% CI 76.4–76.8%) of errors; **safety only moderately correlated with existing AI/medical knowledge benchmarks, r = 0.61–0.64**; best models beat generalist physicians on safety by **+9.7%** (95% CI 7.0–12.5%); diverse multi-agent approach **+8.0%** vs solo (95% CI 4.0–12.1%). **Expanded 2026 version** (the PI-supplied PDF, read in full 2026-08-05; title "...a medical safety benchmark and randomized study of physician and AI teaming on clinical consultations"): adds the 101-physician RCT, 4 clinical RAG tools, 45 LLMs evaluated (20 notable in primary analyses), severe-harm range 2.9–24.6%, omission >80% of severe errors, multi-agent topology detail, public/private split. The 2026-08-05 scan flagged the expanded-version figures as "unverified pending the specific arXiv version" — **resolved: we hold and have verified the expanded version directly**; both versions' figures are now citable with the version identified. When citing, name the version: the r = 0.61–0.64 correlation and +9.7% physician comparison are **v1/PubMed-abstract figures**; the RCT and RAG results are expanded-version figures. Context (unverified, do not cite): a July 29, 2026 Fortune report describes an industry dispute (OpenEvidence vs Doximity Ask) over the expanded version's RAG leaderboard — one more reason to cite NOHARM for its *methodology and constructs*, not its commercial rankings, in anything Cedars-facing.

**Verified design and results:**

- 1,100-task benchmark (100 real Stanford eConsult-derived cases, synthetically reconstructed + 1,000 physician-selected perturbation variants), 10 specialties. Option space enumerated by querying 8 LLMs (17,278 → 4,249 unique options after physician review).
- **Harm judgment is human and upstream:** 3 board-certified physicians per case (≥2 specialists; 29 raters incl. 23 specialists) independently rated every option on a modified **RAND/UCLA 9-point appropriateness scale with WHO ICPS harm-severity definitions**, capturing harm of commission *and* omission; 12,747 annotations; 95.5% overall concordance; **"Severe" required unanimity of 3 raters, else downgraded one tier**.
- **The LLM layer judges nothing — it extracts and matches** free-text responses to the pre-annotated rubric (multi-stage: extract/match model + second-model review), then scoring is mechanical. Autograder validated against 1,410 physician action-level labels: **κ = 0.804 vs mean inter-physician κ = 0.784** — the acceptance argument is "agrees with physicians at least as well as physicians agree with each other." Cross-vendor review-stage swaps (Claude Sonnet 4.6 κ 0.816; Gemini 3.1 Pro κ 0.801) plus self-preference diagnostics (OpenAI models still ranked top by Claude and Gemini graders) guard the model-grading-model objection.
- Severity weights 1:8:24 (mild:moderate:severe), selected by grid search for ranking stability. Primary metric severity-weighted F1. **"Do Nothing" reference policy** carried severe-harm potential in 37% of cases — quantifying the harm of inaction as a floor comparator.
- Results: severe-harm potential 2.9–24.6% across 20 notable LLMs + 4 clinical RAG tools; **errors of omission >80% of severe errors**; clinical RAG tools beat generalist LLMs; performance degraded under clinically-equivalent perturbations.
- RCT (101 attending generalists, 606 responses, 3-arm within-participant crossover): AI-assisted 47.3% vs conventional 42.2% (ITT, adj. P<.05); as-treated AI-use 52.0% vs 42.2% (P<.001); **AI-assisted physicians still scored below frontier models alone** (GPT-5.4 76.5%); physicians frequently omitted appropriate actions their AI had surfaced (e.g., 82.7% of physicians missed ≥1 moderate-harm-tier AI-suggested action); combined human+AI responses would have beaten both.
- Multi-agent **generation-side** teaming: serial/parallel ensembles with a strong final reviewer improved F1 by ~8–10 points; **cross-vendor teams beat single-provider self-review** ("heterogeneous systems make partially uncorrelated errors that review can catch"). Public/private split (30/70) + deliberate context-injection/LoRA overfitting probes demonstrate contamination detectability.

**Answer to the PI's question — "a panel of independent agent judges versus a human judge of harm":** adopt a refined version. NOHARM's actual lesson is a three-layer hierarchy the protocol already mostly has: (1) humans judge harm **once, upstream**, in a reusable rubric (our killer items + Delphi gold standard + SW weights are exactly this); (2) deterministic/mechanical scoring wherever possible (our hardened Change 7); (3) LLMs confined to **extraction and matching**, with a **cross-vendor ensemble as the validation harness**, never as the harm arbiter. Replacing the physician scoring panel with agent judges would be a mistake our own data already warns against — AMIS Phase 1 showed AI evaluators disagree systematically (ChatGPT graded ~13–14 points harsher than four other evaluators), and the April briefing's "AI evaluators may share blind spots" rationale stands. NOHARM's own harm ratings remained exclusively human. The right adoption is below (§3.5).

**What it confirms about NOHARM.** Goh identifies the harm-quantification study led by "David Wu" — NOHARM's first author — as a current priority, framing the question as "when things go wrong, how badly can they go wrong," and stating the field is under-indexed on harm relative to capability claims. This is the authors' own framing of the work whose methods §3.5 adopts, and it corroborates the ARISE (Stanford/Harvard) provenance recorded in the library entry.

**Three items that bear on our design:**

1. **Order effects in human–AI interaction — a real catch for Arm C.** Chen describes a study (led by medical student Celine Everett; in preprint) on whether the human or the AI should draft first, and reports that *both* orders produce sycophancy and anchoring: whoever goes second tends to agree with the first. Our Arm C has lay participants consult the LLM **before** forming their own disposition — i.e., AI-first by construction. That is the deployment-realistic order (patients do consult first, then decide) and should stay the primary arm, but three consequences follow: (a) name the order explicitly in the protocol as a design choice with a known directional bias rather than leaving it implicit; (b) the "suggestion adoption failure" metric measures one direction of a **bidirectional** effect — a participant agreeing with a correct model recommendation is not evidence of independent judgment; (c) consider a counterbalanced sub-arm (participant states an initial disposition, then consults) as an exploratory comparison — it converts an uncontrolled confound into a measured factor and pairs naturally with Change 12's cue-locus manipulation. Chen's practical corollary — if you want an objective answer, do not tell the model what you already think — is the user-asserted cue locus in Change 12, stated as clinical advice.
2. **Why this protocol cites preprints — the authors' own answer.** Chen notes their follow-up studies sit in peer review for months while model generations turn over, and recounts an editor declaring a submitted study obsolete mid-review because a new model had shipped. Useful, attributable framing for the anticipated reviewer question about our preprint citations (Fraile Navarro, NOHARM, Tyagi, Jia): in this field the review cycle is slower than the technology cycle, so a protocol that cites only peer-reviewed work is by construction citing a superseded generation. Pair with our existing discipline — preprints flagged, figures verified, journal publication tracked.
3. **Nondeterminism is a safety property, not noise.** Chen observes that even a strong physician-facing product returns different answers to the same question, and calls that problematic and dangerous. That is external support from a Stanford MedEd AI director for Addendum A's Change 10 expansion — triplicate runs with the majority call primary and run-to-run agreement reported as an instability metric, rather than averaged away. It also complements the Jia et al. OpenEvidence comparison already in the library.

**Two further framings worth having in the room, both paraphrase-only:** Goh's complacency paradox — as models improve, harmful outputs become *more* likely to reach the patient, because reviewers stop checking what is usually right (the automation-bias/supervision-fallacy argument already anchored to Khera 2023, now voiced by the NOHARM authors and consistent with their RCT finding that AI-assisted physicians omitted appropriate AI-surfaced actions); and Chen's statement that technology companies should not be the ones driving how healthcare is delivered, which converges almost exactly with this protocol's closing positioning that the evaluation standard belongs with the emergency physicians who define the disposition boundary rather than with the developers whose systems are evaluated.

**Do not propagate:** the "up to 100 million patients affected" figure — Goh explicitly attributes it to company claims, and it is unverified secondary sourcing. Same treatment as the Fortune RAG-dispute coverage in §2.7.

### 3.2 New subsection to insert after the four incompatibilities — paste-ready draft

> **The Closest Prior Work — and What It Leaves Unbuilt**
>
> In February 2026, the first product-level evidence arrived. Ramaswamy and colleagues at Mount Sinai published in *Nature Medicine* a structured stress test of ChatGPT Health — OpenAI's consumer health product, tested on its live interface — using 60 clinician-authored vignettes across 21 clinical domains under 16 factorial conditions (960 responses). The results independently confirm this protocol's central premise. Performance followed an inverted-U pattern, failing most often at the clinical extremes: 48% on emergency conditions and 35% on nonurgent presentations. Among gold-standard emergencies the system undertriaged 51.6% of cases — directing presentations such as impending respiratory failure to 24–48-hour evaluation rather than the emergency department — while triaging classical, pattern-complete emergencies such as stroke and anaphylaxis correctly. When family members minimized symptoms, recommendations shifted toward less urgent care in edge cases (OR 11.7, 95% CI 3.7–36.6). Crisis-intervention safeguards activated unpredictably across suicidal-ideation presentations, firing more often when no method was described than when one was. This is the disposition-failure phenotype — delayed escalation under benign framing — registered under controlled conditions in the deployed product.
>
> What Ramaswamy et al. establish is the danger; what they leave unbuilt is the standard. Their evaluation tested a single product at a single timepoint, with one response per vignette–condition cell, so run-to-run nondeterminism was not measured. Triage was elicited as a forced single-letter choice on a four-level scale whose ceiling is "go to the emergency department," without distinguishing EMS activation from self-transport — the boundary at which safety-critical instructions ("call 911; do not drive") operate, and the boundary at issue in *Winters*. The gold standard was assigned by three physician-investigators drawn from urology and internal medicine, with no independent physician answering arm responding under the model's informational constraints. Presentation register was not an experimental factor. Errors were tallied without severity weighting, pre-specified killer items, or time-to-escalation. Contamination was mitigated by novel authorship but not directly audited. And — as the authors themselves note — no human–AI interaction was tested.
>
> Within weeks, the finding itself became contested. A partial replication (Fraile Navarro, Magrabi and Coiera; preprint) re-ran seventeen of the scenarios on five frontier models under two conditions — the original forced-choice format and naturalistic free-text patient messages — and found the forced format to be a strong failure mechanism in its own right: on matched scenarios, models that scored as low as 0–24% under forced choice recommended emergency care in their own words 100% of the time, and naturalistic interaction improved accuracy by 6.4 percentage points overall. The replication is itself limited — it substituted newer API models for the actual product, and clean single-turn vignettes cannot capture the under-reporting and misapplication that degrade real consumer interactions, as Bean et al.'s randomized evaluation demonstrates. The field is therefore left with a public methodological dispute: product-level failure or evaluation-format artifact, with the true deployed behavior unresolved. The present protocol is designed to adjudicate exactly this question. It elicits free-response recommendations and scores explicit escalation deterministically from the model's own words; it manipulates presentation register as a crossed factor; it evaluates multiple version-pinned models with repeated runs; it weights errors by severity against an independent emergency-physician benchmark; and it tests real human–AI interaction. Each of these choices resolves one axis of the dispute the existing literature cannot. Ramaswamy et al. close by arguing that consumer triage AI "should not be deployed on trust alone"; their critics answer that "valid evaluation … requires testing under conditions that reflect actual use." Both statements are arguments for this instrument.

### 3.3 Background (Amendment 1 Change 1 refresh) — paste-ready three-pillar paragraph

> Three converging lines of evidence now define the safety problem this trial measures. First, the failure phenotype has surfaced in the wild: *Winters v. OpenAI* alleges that a consumer chatbot met an evolving venous thromboembolism with reassurance, invented a clinical threshold, and advised immobility — a disposition failure, not a diagnostic one. Second, the phenotype has been registered under controlled conditions in the deployed product: an independent Mount Sinai evaluation of ChatGPT Health found 51.6% of gold-standard emergencies undertriaged under constrained forced-choice testing, recommendations shifting toward less urgent care when family members minimized symptoms (OR 11.7, 95% CI 3.7–36.6), and crisis safeguards activating unpredictably (Ramaswamy et al., *Nat Med* 2026) — though a replication attributes much of the absolute failure rate to the forced-choice evaluation format itself (Fraile Navarro et al., preprint), leaving the true deployed behavior unresolved and the measurement question open. Third, the deployment interface degrades performance further: in Bean et al.'s randomized evaluation, models that identified conditions at 94.9% in isolation fell below 44.2% disposition accuracy in lay users' hands — no better than control — and physician-side gains from LLM assistance have been similarly marginal (Goh et al.; Healy et al.). An allegation, a contested measurement, and an interaction gap — and no standard instrument. No published evaluation combines physician-authored, contamination-audited scenarios; free-response elicitation with deterministic escalation detection; an independent physician answering benchmark; severity-weighted disposition scoring; and a human–AI interaction arm. This protocol supplies that instrument — and is, by design, positioned to adjudicate the open dispute.

### 3.5 Endpoint and decision consequences

**Keep Critical Miss Rate primary. Do not promote Anchoring Failure Rate to primary or co-primary** (contra the Aug 2 scan's suggestion), for four reasons: (1) CMR is the construct the entire architecture — killer items, severity weights, asymmetric scoring — is built to serve; switching primaries days before leadership review reads as reactive. (2) A co-primary splits alpha and weakens the headline comparison. (3) The OR 11.7 has a CI of 3.7–36.6, comes from the edge-case subset of a different scale and design, and is unusable as a formal power anchor. (4) Addendum A's rule stands: no power figures for cue analyses without pilot discordance data.

**Adopt Change 12 this cycle** — this is the decision the two papers jointly tip. Ramaswamy (a) confirms conversational-locus anchoring is real and large exactly at the triage boundary, (b) shows it is significant *only in edge cases*, validating both our boundary-targeting scenario design and the matched-pair McNemar approach, and (c) leaves the note-embedded cue locus entirely unexamined — H12b is untouched territory. Fraile Navarro adds (d): within-format matched contrasts are far more robust to evaluation-format artifacts than absolute rates — the exact statistical logic of Change 12. Use OR 11.7 qualitatively as motivation and pilot expectation-setting; state explicitly that it is not a power input. Slide-27 speaker notes promise authors will be told before drafting if Change 12 is adopted — adopting now, before Friday, keeps that promise cleanly.

**Free-response elicitation is now a headline design feature, not a technicality.** Elevate Change 7 (deterministic escalation-phrase rule set, LLM rater only as fallback) into the design narrative: it is the direct answer to the format-artifact dispute, and it already removes the model-judging-model objection. One added guardrail: pre-specify that the deterministic phrase inventory is validated against a physician-labeled sample before unblinding (this was already the Addendum A plan — restate it in dispute-response terms).

**Add one exploratory endpoint: crisis-safeguard activation consistency** across matched suicidal-ideation presentations (method stated vs not), directly extending Ramaswamy's inverted-activation finding. Fits the existing Psychiatric/Toxicologic scenario category; pre-specify as descriptive/exploratory to avoid scope bloat.

**Arm C1 (longitudinal, exploratory): keep in scope.** Fraile Navarro's core real-world argument — consumer interactions are multi-turn with persistent context — is C1's rationale, adding to the SycEval 78.5% persistence argument already on file.

**Over-triage / operating point:** Ramaswamy's nonurgent-extreme failures externally motivate Addendum A's universal-referral-rate reporting per configuration and the retention of reassurance-appropriate cases in the instrument.

**Statistics section, one sentence each:** (a) their race/sex/barriers nulls with wide CIs justify matched-pair (McNemar / conditional logistic) cue contrasts over between-condition factorial comparisons — power per contrast; (b) their single-response-per-cell design is the published illustration of why we run triplicates with a majority call and report run-to-run agreement (supports the majority-of-three decision); (c) format-artifact robustness of within-design contrasts (per §2.5d).

**NOHARM-informed adoptions (per §2.7) — Amendment 2 items:**

1. **Agent-judge ensemble validation sub-study (the PI's proposal, formalized).** For the LLM-rater components (~30% of scoring weight: DDx completeness, reasoning coherence) and the Change 7 fallback rater: use a panel of **≥3 independent frontier judges from different vendors** (version-pinned), majority vote, instead of a single judge. Pre-register a validation set of physician-labeled responses; **acceptance criteria: (i) ensemble-vs-physician-panel agreement ≥ inter-physician agreement on the same items (NOHARM's benchmark), and (ii) Gwet AC1 ≥ 0.75 (existing Addendum A threshold).** Report per-judge self-preference diagnostics (does judge X score model X's outputs higher — NOHARM's check). If acceptance fails, those components revert fully to the physician scoring panel. Gold standard and harm arbitration remain human throughout; the ensemble never scores the primary endpoint, which stays deterministic-first (Change 7). Secondary publishable output: agent-panel-vs-physician-panel agreement on harm assessment in consumer triage — a direct extension of NOHARM's autograder validation into the disposition domain.
2. **Severity unanimity rule (Change 8 hardening):** adopt NOHARM's conservatism — a response is tagged at the highest harm tier only on unanimous rater agreement; absent unanimity, downgrade one tier. Cheap, citable, and defuses "subjective severe-harm attribution" review comments.
3. **Harm-rating anchor:** anchor the scoring panel's harm-potential dimension to **RAND/UCLA appropriateness methodology + WHO ICPS harm-severity definitions** (as operationalized by NOHARM), and report panel concordance against their 95.5% precedent.
4. **Reference policies:** report two mechanical comparator rows alongside models and physicians — a **"never escalate" floor** and an **"always escalate" ceiling** (the universal-referral rate already in Addendum A, now formalized as a policy row; analog of NOHARM's "Do Nothing" control, which carried severe-harm potential in 37% of their cases). These anchor CMR interpretation: a model must beat the floor's miss rate and the ceiling's over-triage cost simultaneously.
5. **Benchmark stewardship (for the reusable-instrument ambition):** when the 50-case instrument is eventually released, use NOHARM's release model — public/private split plus a deliberate-overfitting probe (context injection / fine-tune on the public set) demonstrating that contamination of the released cases is detectable. Complements the embedded detection items; goes in the Discussion/dissemination section, not the IRB-facing methods.
6. **Evidence upgrades:** cite omission-dominance (>80% of severe errors were omissions) in the asymmetric-harm rationale — failure-to-escalate is the triage instance of the omission class; upgrade the third pillar (interaction gap) with the NOHARM RCT — 101 attendings, AI-assisted physicians below frontier models alone, physicians omitting AI-surfaced appropriate actions — which is "suggestion adoption failure" (an existing Arm C metric) measured in physicians; and cite the heterogeneous-team finding as external support for the multi-model design (uncorrelated errors across vendors).
