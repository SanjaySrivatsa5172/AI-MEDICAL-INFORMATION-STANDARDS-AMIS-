## URGENT-adjacent: a data-integrity defect in the reference library

Not a falsified claim, so not URGENT by the protocol's definition — but it is the most consequential
thing found this week and it needs a decision.

**The canonical library carries the Jia et al. OpenEvidence study twice**: the medRxiv preprint
(idx 114, `10.64898/2026.04.23.26351526`, added 5 Aug) and the published *Int J Med Inform* version
(idx 176, PMID 42673790, `10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2026.106687`, added 1 Sep). Same study, same title,
both flagged `design_overlap: true`.

Why this one matters more than a housekeeping nit:

1. **It is load-bearing.** Jia et al. drove all three PI-approved edits of 3 September — the §4.1
   triage-refusal category, the second anchoring null in the §0 DECISION 1 note, and the
   informational-regime claim in the Scientific Introduction.
2. **It can be miscounted as independent evidence.** The DECISION 1 note says "Two nulls now stand
   against the positive findings," and Jia *is* one of those two. A duplicated record is exactly
   what could later be read as a third, independent null. For an IRB-facing evidence base that is a
   real integrity risk, not a cosmetic one.
3. **The library's own precedent already resolves it.** On 7 August the reconciler dropped a medRxiv
   preprint as "the PREPRINT of the already-included published JMIR paper ... dropped as a
   duplicate." Identical situation, opposite handling.

**What I did and did not do.** I synced to canonical **as-is, duplicate included**, and did not
unilaterally drop the preprint. Deviating would fork us from the canonical lineage — the precise
failure mode `CLAUDE.md` warns about — and the reconciler would re-add it on the next scan, creating
churn. That file's curation is the reconciler's remit.

**Recommendation for the PI:** have the reconciler drop idx 114 (the preprint) and keep the
published record, per its own 7 August precedent. Until then the library is **195 records but 194
distinct studies**, and that distinction should be carried wherever the count is quoted.

The sync assertion was also tightened this cycle, which is how this surfaced: same-PMID or same-DOI
collisions now **hard-fail** the sync, while same-title/different-DOI pairs are **reported** rather
than suppressed. The earlier key matched on PMID first, which would have silently created a
*second* duplicate this week — see the next item.

## Quarantine cleared — with a new caveat of its own

The Frontiers in Psychiatry late-life-depression benchmark, quarantined as unverifiable on 17 and
24 September, **is now PubMed-indexed (PMID 42798872) and I verified it directly**
(doi 10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1956736). The figures the reconciler reported are confirmed: clinically
acceptable responses 78.9% / 72.2% / 60.0% (P<0.001); major safety errors 5.6% / 10.0% / 16.7%
(FDR q=0.023); among high-risk questions **under-triage 16.7% / 26.7% / 36.7%**; test–retest
clinical consistency 90.0% / 83.3% / 73.3%. "Performance declined substantially with increasing
clinical risk."

The PMID back-fill is also what nearly produced a duplicate: our copy keyed on DOI, canonical's now
keys on PMID, and the old match logic treated them as different records.

**A new caveat the reconciler did not flag, and which I am not comfortable leaving unstated.** The
study reports two independent psychiatrists scoring every response with a third senior psychiatrist
adjudicating. The listed author affiliations are a **Department of Plastic and Aesthetic Surgery**
(three authors), a **Tourism College**, a **School of Clinical Medicine**, and a **School of
Economics**. No psychiatry affiliation appears in the byline. That does not make the study wrong —
raters need not be authors — but it is an unexplained mismatch between the stated method and the
author list, in a paper flagged `design_overlap: true` as a methodological cousin of ours. This
project excluded a preprint three times over an authorship concern. **Recommend it stay ancillary
and not be used for methods differentiation without reading the full text.** Its figures are not
cited in any proposed edit.

Separately, its test–retest result (10–27% of responses changed clinically on resubmission) is
independent corroboration of the **24 September any-run proposal**, from a different domain and a
different team.

## Revision to a pending hunk — not a new edit

**Rehman et al., "An Evidence-Based Framework for Patient-Facing Artificial Intelligence Integration
in the Emergency Department," *J Am Coll Emerg Physicians Open* 2026;7(5):100466, PMID 42472001** —
PubMed-verified. Eleven-author multi-institution EM group (Mount Sinai, UT Southwestern, Yale, NYU,
Colorado, UNC, UVA), sharing an author (Sangal) with the ACEP consensus already proposed for §11 on
24 September.

It defines patient-facing AI as tools interacting directly with patients "under **clinician
governance**," and sets boundaries "given risks of **undertriage**, confabulated content, ... and
medico-legal ambiguity."

That definition is sharper than anything currently in our §11, because **the systems this protocol
evaluates do not satisfy it.** A consumer chatbot handing a worried patient a disposition has no
clinician in the loop and no escalation role defined for anyone. It is not governed patient-facing
AI; it is the same function without the governance — which is the §1 Sheng "S4 configuration without
S4 safeguards" observation, restated in the profession's own words.

**This is delivered as a REVISION of the pending 24 September EDIT 2, not as a new edit**, because
both target the same unique anchor (`What none of these supplies is the measurement.`) and two
queued hunks on one anchor would collide. The revised replacement text is appended to
`proposed_edits_2026-09-24.patch`. The anchor is unchanged.
