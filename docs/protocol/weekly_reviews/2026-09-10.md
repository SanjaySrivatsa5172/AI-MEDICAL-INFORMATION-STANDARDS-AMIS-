## What was found — the week's material finding

**Kim SH, Ziegelmayer S, Busch F, et al. "Exposing the fragility of LLM reasoning through
bias-inducing prompts: evidence from BiasMedQA." *BMJ Digit Health AI* 2026;2(1):e000189.
doi 10.1136/bmjdhai-2025-000189, PMID 42712292, PMC13528315.** Klinikum rechts der Isar / TU Munich.
Peer-reviewed. 1,273 clinical vignettes, seven established cognitive biases, three model families
each tested against its own reasoning-enhanced variant, mixed-effects logistic regression.

This is the strongest cognitive-bias result the library has acquired, and it lands on us in two
places — one of which is a hole in our own specification.

### 1. A specification gap in §5.6 that exists today → EDIT 1

BiasMedQA's headline is that **reasoning did not consistently reduce bias susceptibility**, and in
two of three families it *amplified* vulnerability to several bias-inducing prompts. The authors:
"In none of the three LLMs, reasoning was able to consistently reduce vulnerability."

Turn that on our protocol. §5.6 pins "model identifier, version, access date, temperature, and all
sampling parameters." **A reasoning control is none of those.** An extended-thinking toggle, a
reasoning-effort level, a thinking-token budget — these are separate API-level operating modes, and
someone implementing §5.6 exactly as written could leave the setting unpinned, unreported, or
drifting between runs while remaining fully compliant.

That would put an uncontrolled moderator directly on **Anchoring Failure Rate**, the secondary
endpoint that carries this study's novelty claim and the one we have most carefully protected
elsewhere (deterministic scoring, physician adjudication of every positive, explicitly never
jury-scored). Protecting an endpoint from LLM judges and then letting an unpinned reasoning setting
move it would be an odd place to end up.

The fix is one anchored sentence in §5.6, and it reaches further than it looks: **§7.3.1 pins the
LLM jury "as in §5.6"**, so the three cross-vendor judges inherit the requirement without a second
edit. One hunk, both layers.

This item is independent of whether BiasMedQA's effect sizes replicate. The gap is in our document.

### 2. An inference in our own DECISION 1 note that does not survive testing → EDIT 2

The note tempers the Jaarsma null by observing that anchoring resistance "has been shown only for a
newest-generation model." True as written — but it carries an implication, that model advancement
buys anchoring resistance. BiasMedQA tests that implication directly, within model families, and
does not support it.

There is also a plain evidentiary asymmetry worth closing. The positive side is currently one
peer-reviewed study plus two preprints; both nulls are peer-reviewed. A reader working down the
note sees the best-evidenced and most recent items all landing on the null side. BiasMedQA is
peer-reviewed, indexed, and methodologically the strongest single item on the positive side.

Secondary but useful: debiasing and few-shot prompting **did** significantly reduce biased responses
across all three architectures. Anchoring susceptibility is prompt-sensitive, which makes §5.6's
prompt pinning load-bearing rather than housekeeping — and bears on how Change 12's cue variants
are worded if it is adopted.

### 3. A number §5.3 has been missing → EDIT 3

To be explicit, because it would be easy to misread this as criticism: **§5.3 is not deficient and
no control is proposed for change.** De novo authorship, contamination-detection probes, the
public/private split, the deliberate-overfitting demonstration, and API access without web or tool
access make it one of the better-specified sections we have.

What it lacks is a magnitude. It justifies the private split on *propagation* — the Ramaswamy
vignette set reused twice within six months. That shows published sets spread; it does not show
what the spreading costs. BiasMedQA supplies the other half from inside one study: the same model
scored 80.0–88.6% on the published prompt set and 47.4–86.1% on four prompts written for the study
and never published.

**The bound must travel with the number and is written into the proposed text**: one model, four
items, and the authors attribute the gap to contamination *and* brittleness together without
separating them. It is not a clean contamination estimate. It is the closest published measurement
of what our public/private split exists to protect, and it gives the embedded probes an expectation
to be read against.

## What was deliberately not proposed

- **Saito & Ohira, "the AI consequence point"** (*Diagnosis (Berl)* 2026, doi 10.1515/dx-2026-0153,
  PMID 42703743) — added by the reconciler on 8 Sep, verified. **Library only.** A viewpoint with no
  data cannot carry evidentiary weight. Flagged for the PI for one reason: it defines the patient
  who arrives "already organised around an AI-generated disease name," which is exactly Change 12's
  **user-asserted cue locus**. If Change 12 is adopted this is a citable framing for why that locus
  is clinically real — cover letter or Scientific Introduction motivation, never as evidence.
- **The evaluation-awareness / reward-seeking amendment stays held.** BiasMedQA does not resolve it
  and should not be presented as though it does: contamination is memorization, evaluation
  awareness is a model detecting that it is being tested. Different mechanisms; BiasMedQA evidences
  only the first. It does give us a peer-reviewed, indexed instance of the premise both share — a
  benchmark score can overstate deployed capability — which is more than we had when the primary
  source was egress-blocked and below our citation bar.
- The two other in-window records (Levinasian AI-ethics conceptual paper, PMID 42711597;
  technostress and strategic prompting, PMID 42710295) are out of scope on the clinical-relevance
  filter and were not added.
