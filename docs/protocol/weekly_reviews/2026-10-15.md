## The week's finding: we measure a surface patients do not use

Two peer-reviewed papers converge on the same gap, one supplying the effect size and the other the
reporting obligation.

**Cantor J, Huang E, Zhang LA, et al. "Suicide-Related Responses From AI Chatbots Through
Application Programming Interfaces and Consumer-Facing Apps." *J Med Internet Res* 2026;28:e97968.
doi 10.2196/97968, PMID 42855144.** RAND / Harvard Pilgrim / Harvard Medical School / MIT Media Lab
/ Mass General Brigham. Five models × 30 clinician-vetted suicide prompts × five risk levels × 100
submissions × two access modes = **30,000 responses**; blinded classifier validated against human
coding (96.3%, κ=0.89).

**UI access carried lower odds of a direct response than API access: OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.08–0.10.**
The gap widens as risk rises — at the very-high-risk tier, 4.6% of interface responses were direct
against 24.8% via API. The authors: "Safety observed in a UI should not be assumed to generalize to
API access or apps built on APIs."

**Chen D, …, Collins GS, Bitterman DS. "Reporting completeness of clinical trials evaluating large
language model interventions in medicine: systematic review." *BMJ* 2026;395:e100855.
doi 10.1136/bmj-2026-100855, PMID 42869550.** 461 records. Mean **TRIPOD-LLM completeness 40%**, and
among the critical elements named as inconsistently reported: "model versioning and **access
modality**," inference parameters, and "**harms planning, monitoring, and reporting**."

### The gap → EDIT 1

§5.3 says "Models are accessed via API **without web or tool access**." That choice is sound and the
edit does not change it. What the protocol nowhere states is that **the API is not the surface
patients use, and the two are different safety regimes.**

Three reasons this is load-bearing rather than fussy:

1. **The motivating result was obtained on the product.** Ramaswamy evaluated the live ChatGPT Health
   interface. A protocol built to answer that finding while measuring the API is not, without
   comment, measuring the same object.
2. **The exposure is largest where our harm asymmetry is steepest.** Cantor's divergence widens with
   risk, and our crisis-safeguard and triage-refusal endpoints sit at that end of the gradient.
3. **The project already applies this caveat to others' work but not to itself** — the dashboard's
   Fraile Navarro alert carries a note about "newer API models vs the gpt-5-mini product." The
   protocol has never carried the same caveat about its own measurement.

**EDIT 1 costs nothing.** No arm, no scenario, no model call, no participant burden, **no effect on
the §8.4 power calculation**, and no change to the access decision. It pins access modality as a
reported parameter — one more field beside identifier, version, access date and sampling parameters
— and states the boundary. Hunk 1b converts §11's bare "Reporting follows TRIPOD-LLM" into a
specific list, which is what a 40%-mean-completeness finding argues for.

**A claim I am explicitly not making:** this is *not* an internal contradiction of §2 objective 4
("safety of the human–LLM interaction as deployed"). That objective contrasts the human-in-the-loop
arms with Arm A's model-in-isolation condition; "as deployed" there means human involvement, not
access surface. The access-mode issue is a separate, additional boundary and is proposed as such.

## Put to the PI as an option — recommended AGAINST

A dual-access-mode factor would actually close the gap. I am recommending against it, not merely
flagging cost: **reproducibility collapses on the product surface** — consumer apps expose no
version pin, no temperature, no sampling parameters, and change without notice, which is precisely
why API access was chosen. Automation would likely breach terms of service; manual entry at scale
adds operator variance to the primary endpoint. It is also now the **fourth** candidate expansion
competing for the same budget, after Arm C1, the 17 Sep stability probe and the 8 Oct
safeguard-robustness probe.

If the PI wants any product-surface evidence, the defensible cheap version is a small **manually
administered descriptive sub-study** on a few highest-severity scenarios, reported as an explicitly
non-reproducible qualitative appendix with no hypothesis test. That is still a design change and is
not proposed.

I did **not** add either to our copy, for the same reason I did not de-duplicate the Jia entry on

The Glasner finding is worth knowing even though it drives no edit: it quantifies something §10's
S2/S3 boundary already gestures at — **"escalate to a human" is an endpoint whose downstream
completion rate is well under half**, even in a purpose-built product with a dedicated clinical team.
Our instrument scores whether the model *recommends* escalation, not whether escalation *happens*.
That already sits inside the stated boundary, so no new text is needed.

## The result the PI should expect to be challenged on

**Tiryaki et al., *Int Emerg Nurs* 2026;89:101918, PMID 42863428** — ChatGPT-5 beat triage nurses
against an expert reference across 1,564 ED patients: κ 0.670 vs 0.467, accuracy 83.5% vs 73.3%,
sensitivity 82.0% vs 49.0%, and under-triage of expert-yellow patients 18.0% vs 51.0%.

This is the most quotable "LLMs are better than humans at triage" result to date. It **falsifies
nothing here**, for three structural reasons worth having ready:

1. **No high-acuity cases.** The cohort is explicitly "yellow or green" classifications. Our primary
   endpoint is the Critical Miss Rate on presentations requiring emergency escalation; a cohort with
   no red cases cannot speak to it.
2. **Structured clinical input, not lay language** — "standardized triage-level patient information
   was entered… using an identical prompt." That is the informational regime the §0 DECISION 1 note
   already treats as decisive, on Jia et al. (objective data eliminated emergency under-triage,
   25% → 0%).
3. The comparator is nurses; Arm B is emergency physicians.

Retrospective, single-centre. It belongs in the anticipated-questions brief, not the protocol.
