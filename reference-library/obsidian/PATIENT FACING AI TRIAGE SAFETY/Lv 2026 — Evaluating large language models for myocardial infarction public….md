# Evaluating large language models for myocardial infarction public health education: a comparative study on information quality, transparency and readability

**Lv T, Bao W, Li S, et al.** — *Frontiers in Public Health* (2026)

> [!note] Clinical relevance
> PHYSICIAN-ADJUDICATED (two cardiologists) safety/quality evaluation of PATIENT-FACING LLM output for MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION -- the archetypal 'is this chest pain an emergency?' scenario -- squarely on the protocol's patient-facing-ai + ai-safety-failure-modes + lay-language axes. This is the direct sibling of the library's existing Zuo et al. TBI/concussion chatbot entry (same journal, Front Public Health; same DISCERN/EQIP/GQS/JAMA instrument suite; blinded/physician raters; an emergency-relevant condition; a named clinical-safety failure mode) and is included on the same ancillary basis. Design: 25 representative MI patient-education questions submitted to Gemini 3.5 Flash, Claude Opus 4.8, and ChatGPT 5.5 (zero-shot, English); output independently scored by two cardiologists on DISCERN, EQIP, GQS, and JAMA PLUS a strict CLINICAL SAFETY ASSESSMENT, with readability measured on six metrics (FRES, ARI, GFI, CLI, FKGL, SMOG). Findings bearing on the protocol's premise: models 'sometimes generated incomplete, incorrect, or even potentially harmful information' and 'occasionally produce clinically inappropriate directives' for an acute, life-threatening emergency condition (a false-reassurance / unsafe-guidance failure mode) -- the exact patient-facing harm the protocol studies; all models failed the JAMA transparency benchmark and none met the recommended sixth-grade readability threshold (a lay-language / health-literacy access barrier, the protocol's everyday-language anchor), leading the authors to conclude current LLMs are 'not yet capable as standalone health education tools for MI.'
