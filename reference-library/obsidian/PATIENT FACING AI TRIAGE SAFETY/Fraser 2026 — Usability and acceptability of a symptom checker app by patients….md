# Usability and acceptability of a symptom checker app by patients seeking urgent care

**Fraser H, Bacher I, Khatri I, et al.** — *Applied Clinical Informatics* (2026)

> [!note] Clinical relevance
> PATIENT-FACING consumer SYMPTOM-CHECKER evaluation in URGENT-care and EMERGENCY-department patients -- squarely on Topic 1 (the 'digital front door': patients enter symptoms in everyday language BEFORE a physician assessment) and the protocol's lay-language anchor. Design: the Ada symptom checker was evaluated for usability/acceptability (Technology Acceptance Model questionnaire) and diagnostic accuracy in 214 urgent primary-care patients, extended with a more diverse 40-patient ED cohort from an earlier study; patients entered their own symptoms into Ada before being seen, and Ada's differential was compared against the treating physician's diagnosis (and re-reviewed by 3 independent physicians). Findings bearing on the protocol: Ada's top-3 diagnoses matched >=1 treating-physician diagnosis in 63.3% (109/172) of primary-care cases with a clear final diagnosis, and independent physicians' median match on Ada summaries was 72.1% (124/172); clinical history was rated complete in only ~two-thirds of cases (an everyday-language history-capture gap). Usability was high overall (73% primary-care / 84% ED agreement on TAM items) but significantly LOWER for patients over 50 and those with limited technology experience -- a digital-divide/access-equity signal on the everyday-language interface -- while sex, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status and education were not significant; the authors (a team including an emergency physician) explicitly call for validation of symptom checkers AND large language models across diverse populations and for study of their effects on patient decision-making and care-seeking. Bears on the protocol's premise that a patient-facing tool's real-world usability and pre-visit diagnostic suggestions shape whether lay users act appropriately in urgent/emergency contexts.
